\subsection{Explanation layer}\label{sec:xai}
\subsubsection{Stability and certification}
Each alert carries a SHAP attribution \cite{lundberg2017}, rendered in plain language and split into evidence for and against the verdict. Under a 1\,\% Gaussian perturbation, SHAP's top-5 features have a Jaccard stability of 0.83 (Spearman $+0.92$; Review~2 quoted 0.87 from an earlier run of the same evaluation, and we report the value the current evaluation script produces), and LIME's \cite{ribeiro2016} 0.27 (Spearman $+0.21$), while the model's own prediction flips in 0\,\% of cases, so the instability belongs to the explainer. Raising LIME's sample count from 1,000 to 12,000 moves its Jaccard only within 0.16 to 0.38 (0.16, 0.38 and 0.33 at 1,000, 4,000 and 12,000 samples), and its apparent fidelity falls from $+0.349$ to $+0.023$ as sampling converges. Repeated over five independent training partitions, SHAP's Jaccard is $0.85 \pm 0.07$ on IoTID20 and $0.90 \pm 0.04$ on Edge-IIoTset against LIME's $0.25 \pm 0.08$ and $0.22 \pm 0.02$, higher in every partition (five pairs cannot give a paired $p$ below 0.0625, so we report the count). LIME was demoted to an internal cross-check. Each SHAP explanation is certified before display by ablating its top-cited features and checking that the decision moves more than for random features; 82\,\% of 40 explanations certify, and the rest are shown labelled unverified rather than hidden.

\subsubsection{Human evaluation of explanation stability}
Certification only helps if a reader notices it. We ran a within-participant reader study with hypotheses fixed before any session: (H1) readers state the reason for a certified alert correctly more often than for an uncertified one; (H2) readers trust uncertified alerts less; (H3) contradictory alerts, whose top-ranked evidence argues against the verdict, lead readers to cite counter-evidence as the reason. Each participant read the same 20 real alerts from the trained ensemble (8 certified, 6 uncertified, 6 contradictory) in a seeded per-participant order, in four rounds of five, and for each one ticked the measurements the alert based its decision on (the five cited measurements plus two distractors the alert never cites), chose an action (act, ask someone to check, ignore), and rated their own confidence and their trust from 1 to 7; time to decision was recorded. A reason is scored correct when the reader selects at least one measurement cited for the verdict and no counter-evidence or distractor, the rule fixed before the study.

Because participants were not on site, sessions were unsupervised and online, with electronic consent and the debrief on the final screen. Two items of the original protocol were dropped to keep a session near 15 minutes: the verdict and confidence-band questions, which every reader answers trivially because every alert is an ATTACK verdict. One response was re-scored from its raw selections because the study app offered a cited measurement as a distractor for one alert; all scores below are recomputed from raw selections with the fixed rule.

Thirteen participants took part (11 undergraduate and 2 postgraduate students; median machine-learning familiarity 3 of 7; median session 14.8 minutes). Table~\ref{tab:reader} gives the results. None of the three hypotheses is supported: no paired comparison of accuracy, trust, self-confidence or time between categories approaches significance (smallest raw $p = 0.055$, every Bonferroni-corrected $p > 0.65$ over 12 tests). Readers acted without escalating on 57\,\% of certified and 41\,\% of uncertified and contradictory responses ($\chi^2 = 9.99$, $p = 0.041$ over responses), but at participant level the difference is not significant (paired Wilcoxon $p = 0.39$). Excluding the two participants who finished in under five minutes leaves every test non-significant.

The null result on H2 is the finding we had marked in advance as the most important possible outcome: readers did not trust an alert less when the system itself labelled its explanation unverified (4.51 against 4.95 of 7). Two further patterns explain why. First, accuracy is bimodal and tracks expertise: four readers stated no reason correctly and five stated at least 16 of 20, and a reader's accuracy correlates with their machine-learning familiarity (Spearman $\rho = 0.86$, $p = 1.4\times10^{-4}$) and with security familiarity ($\rho = 0.61$, $p = 0.027$). The three readers who acted on most certified alerts and on no uncertified one were all among the most accurate, so the certification banner was used only by readers who could already read the evidence. Second, 137 of the 146 incorrect answers (94\,\%) included a measurement the alert listed as evidence \emph{against} its verdict: novice readers treat every listed measurement as a reason. Self-confidence did not follow accuracy, staying between 5.3 and 5.6 of 7 in every category.

These results change the design, not only the paper. Showing evidence for and against in one ranked list, which a specialist reads as balance, misleads a non-specialist, and a text banner does not change how much a non-specialist trusts an alert. An operator-facing view should show only the evidence for the verdict by default and make an unverified explanation change what the operator can do, for example by requiring escalation, rather than adding a line of text. With 13 student participants in unsupervised sessions this is a pilot: it shows how non-specialists use the explanations, not how security operators or clinicians would.

\noindent\textbf{Who noticed the label.} The free-text answers to ``which alerts looked less reliable, and what told you'' were coded independently by two team members (noticed the label, partly, did not, blank) and, as a check that anyone can rerun, by a fixed rule: an answer counts as noticing the label if it mentions the UNVERIFIED status or that removing the cited measurements barely changed the decision. The two coders agreed on all 13 answers (Cohen's $\kappa = 1.00$; 7 answers were not blank), and both matched the rule on every answer. Five of the 13 readers noticed it, two answered without mentioning it (one of whom read it backwards, taking a decision that barely changes as a sign of reliability), and six left the question blank. The five who noticed trusted certified alerts 1.65 points more than uncertified ones and acted on 65\,\% of certified but 13\,\% of uncertified alerts; the eight who did not showed no difference ($-0.32$ points) and acted on 52\,\% of certified and 58\,\% of uncertified alerts. With five and eight readers the difference is not significant (Mann-Whitney $p = 0.23$), but it explains the null result: the label works for the minority who read it, and is ignored or misread by the rest.
